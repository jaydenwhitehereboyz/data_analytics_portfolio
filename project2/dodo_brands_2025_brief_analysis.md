

# **Dodo Brands (DP Global Group) — FY2025 Brief analysis**

*A quarterly/annual earnings review based on the Q4 2025 results release published 12 March 2026\.*

## **Headline**

In Q4 2025, Dodo posted strong growth: global system sales (GMV) rose 18% year-over-year to nearly RUB 38.7 billion. This was driven by 81 net new units opened in Q4 (plus \+310 for the full year — nearly 20% of the total 1,573-store base). Separately, Q4 system-wide order count and average ticket grew \+15.3% and \+2.4% year-over-year (blended across delivery and in-store; Q4 2025 vs Q4 2024).

## **Revenue Mix**

The two largest revenue lines are Retail sales (RUB 6.34bn) and Royalties (RUB 6.18bn) — together about 69% of total FY2025 revenue. For context, total System Sales (GMV) across the whole network was RUB 144.0bn for FY2025; royalties are the fee Dodo collects on this GMV, not the franchisees' own revenue.

## **Profitability**

In FY2025, revenue grew \+29.0% while operating expenses grew \+22.0%, driving Operating Profit to RUB 1.77bn (+176.6% YoY). Net finance costs (RUB 0.5bn of finance costs, partly offset by RUB 0.18bn of finance income) brought Profit Before Tax down to RUB 1.45bn.

Operating margin roughly doubled year-over-year (4.6% → 9.8%), while Adjusted EBITDA margin rose more modestly (7.6% → 9.0%). The gap is explained by a one-off: the company changed its accounting estimate for franchise fee recognition, adding RUB 623m to Operating Profit — a gain that's explicitly stripped out of Adjusted EBITDA.

## **Cash Generation**

Arguably the most notable achievement in FY2025 is Free Cash Flow: nearly RUB 1bn (RUB 999m), up from negative RUB 0.47bn in FY2024 — a full swing from cash burn to cash generation. Net cash (ex IFRS 16\) stood at RUB 1.67bn as of 31 December 2025, more than double the RUB 0.82bn a year earlier.

## **Franchise Model**

Dodo's business model runs mostly on franchising. Within the Eurasia segment (Russia, Kazakhstan, Belarus) — Dodo Pizza's core market — there are 41 corporate stores (3%) and 1,225 franchised stores (97%) as of year-end 2025\.

To become a franchisee, an entrepreneur needs initial capital (\~RUB 6–15m *estimated*, depending on format, with at least \~70% expected to be own funds rather than a bank loan — *unverified for the Russian market specifically*), a franchise fee (\~RUB 0.35m *estimated*), and ongoing royalties. Royalty terms confirmed via public franchise listings: 3.5% of sales in year one, rising to 5% of sales from year two onward — collected on an ongoing (monthly) basis.

## **Open Question: Why Not Go (Almost) Pure Franchise?**

If franchising is this profitable per unit and requires far less capital from the company, why not shrink corporate ownership toward something like a 99.999% franchise / 0.001% corporate structure?

Some possible reasons (my own reasoning — not stated by the company in this release, so treat as hypotheses to validate, not fact):

1. **R\&D / testing.** Founders may need corporate stores to trial new formats or menu items without pushing execution risk onto franchisees who paid for an already-proven model.  
2. **Operational feel.** Leadership arguably needs to run real stores to manage the system credibly, and strong corporate-store performance serves as a visible proof point for prospective franchisees.  
3. **Training ground.** Corporate stores can double as a place to train new managers or pilot processes before rolling them out system-wide.  
4. **Cash flow timing** — *this specific rationale doesn't hold up*: royalties are collected monthly, not annually, so "corporate stores for steady daily income" isn't really necessary on cash-timing grounds alone.

Worth noting: I initially tried to back this question with a "corporate stores cost \>RUB 10bn" estimate built from the group's consolidated opex lines (raw materials, employee benefits, D\&A). That doesn't hold up — those are group-wide figures covering all 1,573 stores, HQ, and supply infrastructure, not costs isolated to the \~80 corporate stores. The release doesn't provide that breakdown, so the honest version of this section is the open question above, not a resolved calculation.

## **Brand Efficiency**

Advertising & distribution spend grew only \+10.5% (to RUB 3.1bn) while GMV grew \+22.4% and total revenue grew \+29.0% — a reasonable signal that brand recognition is doing more of the work than paid marketing. (Revenue growth is the less clean comparison of the two, since it includes this year's one-off \+623m franchise-fee accounting adjustment; GMV growth is the cleaner read on organic demand.) The resulting free cash flow (\~RUB 1bn) gives Dodo optionality to fund its own commissary/production facilities, international expansion, or subsidies for franchisees in remote locations.

## **Glossary**

***GMV / System Sales*** — Total sales generated by all stores in the network (corporate \+ franchised) to end customers, including VAT. This is the top-line measure of consumer demand across the whole system; it is *not* the company's own revenue, since most of it belongs to franchisees.

***Comparable (Like-for-like) Sales Growth*** — Sales growth measured only across stores that have been open for at least 12 full months, so the number isn't inflated by newly opened locations. Isolates organic, same-store demand growth.

***Royalties*** — The ongoing fee franchisees pay the parent company, calculated as a percentage of their own sales. This is *revenue for the company*, but only a small slice of what franchisees actually generate.

***Retail Sales*** — Revenue the company books directly, typically from corporate-owned stores and/or product sales to the network (e.g. ingredients/supplies), as opposed to fees collected from franchisees.

***Marketing Fee*** — A separate percentage-of-sales contribution franchisees pay into a shared marketing/advertising fund, usually earmarked for brand-wide campaigns rather than general company use.

***Franchise Fee*** — A one-time (or near one-time) payment a franchisee makes to acquire the right to open a new location, distinct from the ongoing royalty.

***Revenue and Income*** — The company's total top-line, summing royalties, retail sales, marketing fee, franchise fee, and other revenue. Larger than any single component but smaller than total System Sales/GMV.

***Operating Profit / Operating Margin*** — Profit after all operating expenses (materials, staff, D\&A, marketing, other opex) but before finance income/costs and tax. Operating margin \= operating profit ÷ revenue.

***Adjusted EBITDA / EBITDA Margin*** — Operating profit adjusted to add back non-cash and non-recurring items (depreciation, amortization, share-based compensation, lease-accounting effects, one-off items) to better reflect underlying, repeatable cash-generating performance. Not an IFRS-defined metric; each company defines its own adjustments.

***Free Cash Flow (FCF)*** — Cash actually left over after operating costs, capital expenditure, working-capital changes, and taxes — i.e., cash genuinely available to the business, as opposed to accounting profit.

***Net Cash (ex IFRS 16\)*** — Cash and equivalents minus financial debt, excluding lease liabilities created by IFRS 16 lease accounting. A cleaner read of balance-sheet financial flexibility.

***Working Capital*** — Short-term operating assets and liabilities (inventory, receivables, payables). A negative change in cash flow terms usually means cash got tied up in the business (e.g., more inventory or receivables) rather than being freed up.

***CAPEX (Capital Expenditure)*** — Cash spent acquiring or upgrading physical assets (property, equipment) and intangible assets (software, etc.).

***Finance Costs / Finance Income*** — Interest and similar expenses/income unrelated to core operations — e.g., interest on debt or lease liabilities (costs), interest earned on cash balances (income).

***Profit Before Tax*** — Operating profit plus/minus net finance income or costs, before income tax is deducted.

***Net Income*** — The bottom line: profit after all expenses, finance costs, and taxes.

***IFRS 16*** — An accounting standard requiring leases (e.g., store rent) to be recognized on the balance sheet as assets and liabilities, rather than treated purely as an operating expense. Companies often report figures "ex IFRS 16" to show results the way they were measured before this rule, for comparability.
